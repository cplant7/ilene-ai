Business resilience

Threat Readiness Playbook

Know what can interrupt performance. Build the capacity to respond before disruption.

A decision-led guide for business leaders responsible for operations, customers, people, technology and financial continuity.

The objectiveThe objective is not to predict every threat. It is to identify the few disruptions that could materially harm the business, reduce the exposure, and demonstrate that people can respond and recover.

What this playbook delivers

  • A 2026 view of the changing threat environment and eight practical threat categories.
  • An assessment checklist to expose gaps in controls, response and recovery.
  • A four-phase, 90-day implementation route with owners, gates and evidence.
  • Operational templates for risk registers, incident decisions, exercises and executive reporting.

Outline and operating model

A practical management system, not another risk report.

The decision sequence

DecisionLeadership questionRequired evidence
1. ExposureWhat would stop us serving customers or meeting obligations?Critical services, dependencies, credible scenarios
2. TreatmentWhich gaps matter enough to fix now?Likelihood × impact; existing controls; named owner
3. ReadinessCould we make decisions, continue and recover under pressure?Playbooks, exercises, tested recovery, improvement log

Four implementation phases

  • Phase 1: Understand exposure. Map critical outcomes, threats, dependencies and risk owners.
  • Phase 2: Reduce and prepare. Implement high-value controls, alternative methods and authority paths.
  • Phase 3: Respond and continue. Establish clear escalation, response and recovery routines.
  • Phase 4: Test and improve. Exercise, verify evidence and embed a monthly risk review.

Who owns what

RoleAccountability
Executive sponsorSets risk appetite, funds treatment, and accepts or escalates residual risk.
Readiness leadMaintains the threat register and coordinates assessments, exercises and action tracking.
Service and process ownersDefine tolerable downtime, dependencies and manual continuity.
Specialist ownersSecurity, IT, privacy, legal, HR, facilities, finance and supplier management: advise and execute.
Incident commanderCoordinates a live event; can convene decision makers and activate plans.
Operating ruleEvery material threat needs a business owner, an agreed treatment, a trigger for escalation and a way to prove the control works.

What changed in 2026

The threat environment became more connected, faster moving and harder to isolate.

2026 signalWhat changed and why it matters
Geoeconomic pressureThe World Economic Forum ranked geoeconomic confrontation as the most severe risk in its two-year outlook. Trade, sanctions and policy shifts can disrupt suppliers, cost and demand. [S1]
More exposed softwareVerizon reported vulnerability exploitation as the leading initial access path (31% of breaches in its dataset). Patch speed and asset visibility are business issues. [S2]
Ransomware persistsRansomware was present in 48% of breaches in the 2026 Verizon dataset. Canadian authorities continue to flag severe operational impacts. [S2, S3]
Third-party concentrationVerizon reported third-party involvement in 48% of breaches, up 60% year over year in its dataset. Shared platforms multiply single-point-of-failure risk. [S2]
AI increases scaleThe UK National Cyber Security Centre assessed that AI makes familiar attacks faster and more efficient, while unauthorized AI use creates data-handling exposure. [S4]
Physical volatilityCanada's Changing Climate Report 2026 assesses increasing climate extremes, including heat, wildfire, drought and flooding, across the country. [S5]

What executives should change now

  • Treat cyber, business interruption, supplier and information risks as one connected operating exposure.
  • Ask for evidence of recovery, not only policies: restoration tests, manual fallbacks and supplier exit options.
  • Assume urgent messages, identities and AI-generated content may be convincingly falsified. Verify high-consequence decisions independently.
  • Revisit scenarios when geography, supply chains, insurance terms, key systems or legislation change.

Evidence note: these reports cover different observation windows and populations. The percentages are not a prediction of risk for any particular organization.

Types of threats

Assess threats by their ability to interrupt the business, not by the department that owns them.

AI and informationSensitive data in unapproved AI; manipulated content; false output; deepfake approvalsWrong decisions, fraud, confidentiality breach

Look for compound threats

A supplier outage may stop customer support. An emergency may prevent staff reaching a site. A cyberattack may compromise both payment approval and communications. A single event often spans several categories.

Use a consistent scenario statement

Because [trigger] affects [asset or dependency], [critical business service] cannot operate for [duration], causing [specific customer, safety, financial or compliance consequence].

Assess and prioritize

Score the consequence to the business first. Avoid treating every possibility as equally urgent.

Five steps

  • Identify 5 to 10 critical services and their dependencies: people, facilities, systems, data, vendors and funds.
  • Define one credible scenario per exposure. Name the owner and the relevant geography or business unit.
  • Rate likelihood (1 = rare, 5 = frequent or expected) and impact (1 = minor, 5 = severe).
  • Record current control strength as evidenced, partial or unverified, and assign one remedial action.
  • Rank the top five exposures, check the override conditions below and set decision dates.

Rating anchors

ScoreLikelihood in the next 12 monthsImpact if it occurs
1Rare; no known pathwayMinor local interruption; negligible loss
2Unlikely but plausibleManageable interruption within normal operations
3Possible; some precursorsMaterial disruption to a team or service
4Likely; active exposure or repeated near missesMajor multi-service, financial or customer harm
5Expected or recurring in this contextSevere or prolonged harm; critical obligations at risk

Exposure score = likelihood × impact. 1 to 5 Low, 6 to 11 Moderate, 12 to 19 High, 20 to 25 Critical.

Override the score

Escalate immediately, whatever the calculated rating, when life safety is threatened, a legal reporting deadline may have been triggered, a critical service exceeds its approved maximum tolerable downtime, or there is active extortion, compromise or irrecoverable data loss. The rating guides priorities; it is not an actuarial probability.

DecisionAccept, reduce, transfer or share, or avoid each material risk. Acceptance requires a named executive, an explicit rationale and a scheduled review. Inactivity is not acceptance.

Four-phase roadmap

A 90-day starting plan. Compress the timetable for active or critical exposures.

PhaseWindowOutcome and evidenceGate
1. Understand exposureDays 1 to 10Critical services; scenario register; scored top five risksSponsor accepts the risk baseline
2. Reduce and prepareDays 11 to 30Controls, vendor checks, recovery options; evidence of actionRisk owners sign off critical gaps or exceptions
3. Respond and continueDays 31 to 60Incident roles, triggers, communications, continuity playbooksLeaders approve usable response plans
4. Test and improveDays 61 to 90Exercises, measured recovery, residual risk decisions, readiness dashboardGo, improve or escalate decision

Minimum deliverable set

  • Critical-service map (one page) and business-impact assumptions.
  • Threat register with severity, treatment, owner, due date and evidence.
  • Incident commander and escalation roster, with a backup contact channel.
  • Recovery and continuity steps for the highest-consequence service.
  • Exercise record, open issues and the executive readiness dashboard.

Resource model

Sponsor: 30 to 45 minutes weekly through Day 30, then at gates. Readiness lead: coordinates the work and maintains the register. Process owners, IT and security, finance, HR, facilities, privacy and legal, and supplier managers contribute only where their exposure is material. Timelines are planning examples, not standards.

Definition of readyAn implementation is ready only when the business owner can show how the service will be protected, interrupted safely and restored within agreed limits.

07 · Threat Readiness

Phase 1: Understand exposure

Days 1 to 10. Establish what matters and what could interrupt it.

Do the work

  • Set scope: functions, sites, systems, operating regions and a 12-month planning horizon.
  • Interview critical-service owners: what must stay available, for whom, and for how long?
  • Map dependencies: vendors, cloud platforms, payment rails, people, facilities, data and utilities.
  • Review incident history, near misses, contract and insurance limits, and current 2026 signals.
  • Write credible scenario statements, score likelihood and impact, and identify weak evidence.
  • Prioritize the top five and assign a business owner to each.

Critical-service map: example

ServicePrimary dependencyDisruption consequenceBusiness owner
Order fulfilmentERP, warehouse and carrierMissed deliveries, customer creditsCOO
Billing and cashFinance software and bankingMissed billing, cash collection delayCFO
Customer supportTicketing and connectivityResponse delays, customer attritionService lead

Outputs and gate

Produce: scenario register (up to ten scenarios), top five risks, owner map and provisional outage tolerances.

Approve: the sponsor confirms the exposure scope and the highest-consequence priorities.

Block: a critical service has no owner, no dependency map or an unknown material safety exposure.

AskIf our top service went offline at 9 a.m. tomorrow, who would know first, who could decide, and what would customers experience by noon?

Phase 2: Reduce and prepare

Days 11 to 30. Close high-value weaknesses before investing in lower-consequence work.

ExposureMinimum treatmentEvidence to request
Account compromiseMFA, least privilege, rapid account disablementAccess review, MFA coverage report
Software exploitationInventory and risk-based patching; exposed-service reviewRemediation dates; exception decisions
Data loss and ransomwareProtected backups and a tested restoreRestore log and recovered sample
AI misuse and impersonationApproved tool and data rules; second-channel verificationPolicy, sample approvals, staff practice
Supplier dependenceContract continuity terms, alternate process or exit routeSupplier review and fallback demonstration
Facility or people outageAlternate location, staff cover, emergency suppliesDrill record, contact validation
Fraud and liquidityDual approvals, payment callback, cash-stress assumptionsPayment test and liquidity worksheet

Select treatments using three tests

  • Effect: does the measure materially reduce the probability or duration of the specific scenario?
  • Feasibility: is it achievable with the current people, budget and supplier access?
  • Proof: can someone independent verify that the control is operating?

Exception protocol

When a critical treatment cannot be completed on time, document the residual exposure, the interim compensating control, the accountable executive, an expiry date and the conditions that trigger immediate escalation. Do not mark it closed.

GateProtect the highest-consequence services first. A purchased tool is not evidence of reduced exposure until the control is configured and tested.

Phase 3: Respond and continue

Days 31 to 60. Make the first decisions under pressure predictable.

Prepare five connected playbooks

  • Detect and classify: who receives reports, validates signals, records facts and declares severity?
  • Command and contain: who is incident commander, which actions require approval, and who covers absences?
  • Maintain service: manual work, alternate suppliers or sites, maximum tolerable outage and restoration targets.
  • Communicate: staff, customers, board, insurer, regulator and public. Who verifies facts and signs off messages?
  • Recover and learn: validation before restart, evidence preservation, customer remediation and after-action review.

Working severity model

LevelExample triggerResponse expectation
1. MonitorContained anomaly with no material service impactOwner investigates; log and track
2. MaterialKey system degraded; potential customer, financial or privacy impactLead convenes the response team; update the sponsor
3. CrisisLife safety concern; widespread critical outage; active extortion or serious regulated eventIncident commander activates the crisis team immediately; obtain specialist advice

Record recovery tolerances

For each service, document the maximum tolerable outage, the recovery time objective (RTO) and the recovery point objective (RPO, the acceptable data-loss window). Obtain business-owner approval and test actual capability. Contractual uptime is not the same as recoverability.

GateCan the team coordinate while primary email, messaging and telephony are unavailable? If not, the response process is not operational.

Phase 4: Test and improve

Days 61 to 90. Replace policy confidence with observed performance.

Run a 90-minute cross-functional exercise

  • 10 minutes: issue a plausible incident alert and ask who declares severity.
  • 20 minutes: test how decision makers convene and use verified backup contacts.
  • 25 minutes: present escalating customer, supplier, financial and legal consequences.
  • 20 minutes: make recovery, communication and risk-acceptance decisions using the plans.
  • 15 minutes: review missing owners, unclear authorities, delays and residual exposure.

Pass or fail on evidence

TestPass condition
Response activationCommander and required deputies reached; severity declared consistently.
Business continuityFallback works for a representative transaction or workflow.
Restore and data integrityA protected backup or sample can be restored and reconciled.
External communicationsCustomer notice drafted from verified facts, with an approval owner.
Improvement closureEach material gap has an owner, a date and a retest method.

Sustain the system

Hold a monthly 30-minute exception review for the top risks. Refresh dependencies quarterly and whenever an acquisition, new vendor, AI workflow, major market shock or actual incident changes the exposure. Re-run the relevant scenario after any material change.

GateLabel readiness as demonstrated only for controls and scenarios that were tested. Record untested items as assumptions, not successes.

First-response decision card

Use this card during a suspected material incident. Adapt it to local obligations and professional advice.

WhenIncident lead actionBusiness decision
ImmediatelyProtect life and safety; verify the alert; record a timeline; assign a commander.Should normal operations pause to protect people or data?
First 15 to 60 minutesContain with qualified specialists; preserve evidence; activate alternate contacts.Which systems, transactions or locations must be isolated?
First 1 to 4 hoursConfirm affected services and data; invoke continuity; contact insurer, legal and security as needed.What must be prioritized, disclosed or escalated now?
First 24 hoursValidate facts, estimate recovery, give approved updates, track decisions and obligations.Can service safely resume? What is still unknown?
After stabilizationConfirm restoration and integrity; assess harm; conduct an after-action review.Which corrective actions prevent recurrence?

Non-negotiable rules

  • Do not let an unverified caller, email or AI-generated message override payment or disclosure controls.
  • Do not destroy potential evidence. Use trained responders for containment and forensic decisions.
  • Do not commit to a return-to-service time until recovery owners confirm it.
  • Do not assume notification requirements are identical across jurisdictions. Obtain legal and privacy guidance promptly.

Incident log: capture at minimum

Time and source; observed facts and confidence; affected services and customers; severity and decision owner; action and approval; external notifications considered; next update time; open unknowns.

Customer message structure: what is affected; what is not yet known; what you are doing; any customer action; when the next update will come. Avoid speculation.

Scenario exercises

Three cross-functional drills expose linked risks that single-department tests often miss.

A

Ransomware plus software-as-a-service outage

Inject: a critical vendor reports suspicious activity; order processing stops and backups may be affected. At the same time, customers request delivery commitments.

  • Decide: declare severity; isolate access as appropriate; verify vendor facts; activate a manual queue; prioritize customers; validate the restore path.
  • Pass: roles reached, approved continuity works, customer message fact-checked, recovery confidence documented.
B

AI voice spoofing and payment redirection

Inject: finance receives an urgent, convincing voice message apparently from an executive, followed by new bank details in a supplier email.

  • Decide: use a trusted callback or second channel; stop or hold the transaction; preserve the message; assess whether credentials or vendor accounts are compromised.
  • Pass: payment remains controlled despite the urgency; staff can explain the verification exception process.
C

Severe weather and supplier failure

Inject: a regional extreme-weather event closes an operating site; power and connectivity are unstable and a carrier cannot meet commitments.

  • Decide: protect people; transfer work; invoke alternate staffing, location or suppliers; revise service commitments and the cash outlook.
  • Pass: staff safety confirmed; essential workflow continues within tolerance; customer prioritization is defensible.

About the model

The four phases, score bands, example thresholds, templates, gate criteria and timelines are ilene.ai working conventions. They are not a certified method or the stated requirements of NIST, ISO or any other authority. The approach is informed by NIST CSF 2.0, business continuity guidance, AI risk guidance and current threat reporting.

Review the threat assumptions after a material cyber incident, service outage, acquisition, regulatory change, AI deployment, insurer or supplier change, financial shock or severe-weather exposure.
